## Russian original

> На Сахалине 5 школ, не считая Дербинской, в которой, за неимением учителя, занятий не было. В 1889—1890 гг. обучалось в них 222 человека: 144 мальчика и 78 девочек, в среднем по 44 на каждую. Я был на острове в каникулярное время, при мне занятий не было, и потому внутренняя жизнь здешних школ, вероятно, оригинальная и очень интересная, осталась для меня неизвестной. Общий голос таков, что сахалинские школы бедны, обставлены нищенски, существование их случайно, необязательно и положение крайне неопределенно, так как никому не известно, будут они существовать или нет. Заведует ими один из чиновников канцелярии начальника острова, образованный молодой человек, но это король, который царствует, но не управляет, так как, в сущности, школами заведуют начальники округов и смотрители тюрем, от которых зависит выбор и назначение учителей. Преподают в школах ссыльные, которые на родине не были учителями, люди мало знакомые с делом и без всякой подготовки. Получают они за свой труд по 10 руб. в месяц; платить дороже администрация находит невозможным и не приглашает лиц свободного состояния, потому что этим пришлось бы платить не меньше 25 руб. Очевидно, преподавание в школах считается занятием неважным, так как надзиратели из ссыльных, которые часто несут неопределенные обязанности и состоят только на побегушках у чиновников, получают по 40 и даже по 50 руб. в месяц[17].
>
> Среди мужского населения грамотные, считая взрослых и детей, составляют 29 %, среди женского — 9 %. Да и эти 9 % относятся исключительно к школьному возрасту, так что о взрослой сахалинской женщине можно сказать, что она грамоте не знает; просвещение не коснулось ее, она поражает своим грубым невежеством, и, мне кажется, нигде в другом месте я не видел таких бестолковых и мало понятливых женщин, как именно здесь, среди преступного и порабощенного населения. Среди детей, прибывших из России, грамотные составляют 25 %, среди же родившихся на Сахалине только 9 %[18].

## English working translation of the supplied Russian

Translated for this project by Codex, 2026-10-19; not a quotation from a published English translation. Historical judgments about women are retained as Chekhov's language and are not assertions adopted by this project.

> There are five schools on Sakhalin, not counting the school at Derbinsk, where no classes were held for lack of a teacher. In 1889–1890, 222 pupils studied in them: 144 boys and 78 girls, an average of 44 per school. I was on the island during the holidays, and no classes were held while I was there; consequently, the internal life of these schools, probably distinctive and very interesting, remained unknown to me. The general consensus is that Sakhalin's schools are poor, furnished in a beggarly fashion, that their existence is a matter of chance rather than an obligation, and that their position is extremely uncertain, since no one knows whether they will continue to exist. They are supervised by an official in the office of the island's governor, an educated young man, but he is a king who reigns without governing, since in practice the schools are run by the district chiefs and prison superintendents, who select and appoint the teachers. Teaching is carried out by exiles who were not teachers in their homeland, people with little knowledge of the work and no training whatsoever. They receive ten roubles a month for their labour; the administration considers it impossible to pay more and does not recruit people of free status, since these would have to be paid at least twenty-five roubles. Evidently, teaching in schools is regarded as an occupation of little importance, since warders recruited from among the exiles, who often have no clearly defined duties and merely run errands for officials, receive forty and even fifty roubles a month.[17]
>
> Among the male population, including adults and children, 29 per cent are literate; among the female population, 9 per cent. Even these 9 per cent belong exclusively to the school-age group, so that it can be said of the adult Sakhalin woman that she cannot read or write; education has not touched her, her crude ignorance is striking, and it seems to me that nowhere else have I seen women so witless and slow to understand as here, among this criminal and enslaved population. Among children who arrived from Russia, 25 per cent are literate, whereas among those born on Sakhalin only 9 per cent are literate.[18]

Translation notes: “per school” renders «на каждую»; it does not mean daily attendance. “People of free status” preserves the distinction in legal status, not a distinction between paid and unpaid workers. “Literate” translates «грамотные»; equivalence with the project's recorded literacy categories still requires review.

## Chapter III: how literacy was elicited

Added 2026-10-19. Attribution supplied by the project owner: Антон Павлович Чехов, *Остров Сахалин (Из путевых записок)*, глава III, description of the tenth census field (literacy). The supplied Russian extract is preserved without translation. Its printed page and edition have not been independently verified in this step.

> В десятой — грамотность. Обыкновенно вопрос предлагают в такой форме: «Знаешь ли грамоте?» — я же спрашивал так: «Умеешь ли читать?» — и это во многих случаях спасало меня от неверных ответов, потому что крестьяне, не пишущие и умеющие разбирать только по-печатному, называют себя неграмотными. Есть и такие, которые из скромности прикидываются невеждами. «Где уж нам? Какая наша грамота?» — и лишь при повторении вопроса говорят: «Разбирал когда-то по-печатному, да теперь, знать, забыл. Народ мы темный, одно слово — мужики». Неграмотными называют себя также плохо видящие глазами и слепые.

Interpretive comments for the literacy analysis:

- Chekhov describes asking whether respondents could read. Writing ability was not required by that question; people able to read printed text but unable to write could describe themselves as illiterate under the broader question.
- Self-description could also be affected by modesty, reported loss of ability and impaired vision. This passage describes questioning and reporting difficulties, not a standardized reading test or a rule for recoding individual records.
- Interpret recorded literacy in this historical reading-focused context. It does not establish writing ability, school attendance or school-entry age. Missing literacy remains distinct from recorded illiteracy.
- The Chapter XIX working translation's phrase “cannot read or write” must not be treated as an operational definition of the census field. The Chapter III passage provides the more specific account of how Chekhov asked the question. Mapping this account to the project's source categories remains subject to source review; no dataset values are changed here.

## Chapter XIX figures and comparison rules

| Reported figure | Population or denominator in this passage | Implication for project comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Five schools | Excludes Derbinsk, where no teacher was available | Not a count of every school building on the island |
| 222 pupils: 144 boys, 78 girls | Pupils in those schools during 1889–1890 | Enrolment/study figure, not the number of literate children in the census |
| Average 44 pupils | Per school; rounded from 222 / 5 | Not daily attendance |
| 29% literate males | Male population, adults and children together | Cannot be compared directly with boys aged 5–15 or with adult men alone |
| 9% literate females | Female population, adults and children together | Not an adult-only or school-age-only rate; the following sentence concerns where literate females occur |
| 25% literate among children arriving from Russia | Children described as arriving from Russia | Exact age range, coverage and category mapping need verification |
| 9% literate among children born on Sakhalin | Children described as born on Sakhalin | Exact age range, coverage and category mapping need verification |

The passage does not specify numeric bounds for “children” or “school age”. Do not assume these mean the project's ages 0–15 or the literacy dashboard's ages 5–15. It does not establish school-entry ages, identify every school as a church school, or measure childhood survival. Chekhov explicitly says he did not observe classes in session.

The project's Recorded origin elsewhere, In transit and Not recorded categories remain distinct. Their combined complement to On Sakhalin is not equivalent to Chekhov's “arrived from Russia” group. Missing literacy remains distinct from recorded illiteracy. These distinctions prevent casual equivalence when reading the historical passage; a numerical reconciliation is not planned.
